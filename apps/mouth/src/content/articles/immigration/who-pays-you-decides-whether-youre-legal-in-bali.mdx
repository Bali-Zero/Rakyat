## The Facts

Indonesia has never operated a formal digital nomad visa, leaving remote workers in a persistent legal grey zone that the country's immigration framework was not designed to accommodate. The question of legality hinges on two variables: the source of one's income and the duration of stay. These two factors determine which visa category applies, what work is permissible under that category, and what enforcement risk an individual carries.

For foreigners paid exclusively by a foreign employer or foreign clients — with no Indonesian-sourced revenue — the dominant interpretation among immigration practitioners has been that such individuals are not performing 'work' on Indonesian soil in the legally actionable sense, since no Indonesian economic entity is their counterparty. This interpretation underpins the widespread use of the B211A Social Cultural Visa and its extensions among digital nomads. However, this reading has never been codified into law, and enforcement has been inconsistent. Immigration officers retain discretionary authority, and 'working' under Indonesian law is defined broadly enough to encompass remote activity if challenged.

The picture changes substantially when any portion of income derives from Indonesian clients or when a foreign national is engaged by an Indonesian company. Under these circumstances, the individual is performing compensated services within Indonesia's commercial ecosystem and is legally required to hold a valid work permit (IMTA) paired with a Temporary Stay Permit (KITAS). Proceeding without these documents constitutes an immigration violation and may carry criminal exposure under the Immigration Law.

Length of stay compounds the risk calculus. Visitors on short tourist entries of a few weeks attract little scrutiny. Those extending stays over multiple months — particularly through visa runs or consecutive extensions — increasingly attract attention from immigration authorities, who have periodically conducted sweeps targeting foreigners suspected of working without authorisation. The longer the residency footprint, the harder it becomes to credibly claim a purely tourist purpose.

Indonesia's Second Home Visa, introduced in 2022, offers a longer-stay option but does not carry an explicit authorisation to earn income from Indonesian sources. It remains a residency instrument, not a work permit. Foreigners using it to service Indonesian clients or accept local employment remain non-compliant unless paired with the appropriate work permit documentation.

### Our Analysis

The framing of 'who pays you' is the right starting point, but it is not the finish line. We regularly see clients who believe they are fully compliant because their salary lands in a foreign bank account — only to discover that attending client meetings in Bali, managing Indonesian staff, or invoicing a Bali-registered entity has quietly shifted their legal exposure. The law does not care where the wire transfer originates; it cares about where the economic activity occurs.

What the guidance tool correctly signals is that there is no single answer. Legality in Bali is situational, and the gap between 'technically fine' and 'legally documented' is where most enforcement risk lives. For short-term stays with 100% foreign-sourced income, the practical risk is low. For anyone building a sustained life in Bali — renting long-term, managing a team, taking on local clients — the informal approach becomes structurally fragile.

Our strong recommendation is to treat the payment-source question as a trigger for a proper compliance review, not a final verdict. The right structure — whether a PT PMA, a Representative Office, or a correctly issued KITAS — costs far less than a deportation and a multi-year ban.

## In Practice

Foreigners receiving income solely from abroad and staying for a matter of weeks face the lowest compliance burden and are largely served by standard tourist or social-cultural visa arrangements, provided they do not interact commercially with Indonesian entities. Those staying for months must confront the question of whether their activity — even if remote — has acquired enough of a local character to trigger permit requirements. Visa runs and consecutive extensions are increasingly scrutinised and are not a compliant long-term strategy. Any foreigner who is effectively living in Bali, regardless of how they entered, should be assessing their situation against permanent residency or structured stay instruments. Those earning any income from Indonesian clients, employed by an Indonesian entity, or managing Indonesian operations are outside the tourist visa framework entirely and must secure proper work authorisation. Failure to do so is not a paperwork technicality — it is an immigration violation that can result in detention, deportation, and re-entry bans of up to five years.
